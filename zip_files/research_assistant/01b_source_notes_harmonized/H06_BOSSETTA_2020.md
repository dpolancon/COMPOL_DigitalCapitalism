# Source Note — LUNA ET AL. 2023 MAYORAL SOCIAL MEDIA CHILE

## Basic information

Author: Juan Pablo Luna, Daniel Alcatruz, Cristian Pérez Muñoz, Fernando Rosenblatt, Sergio Toro Maureira, and Sebastián Valenzuela
Year: 2023
Title: Local Government, Social Media and Management of COVID-19: The Case of Chilean Mayoral Communication
Journal / book / source: Political Communication
PDF filename: PDF006
Source ID: PDF006

## What this source is about

This study looks at how mayors in Chile used social media (Facebook, Twitter, and Instagram) during the COVID-19 pandemic. It found that a town's poverty level mattered much more than the mayor's political party when it came to how they used these platforms. In poorer areas, social media became a vital "digital lifeline" for the state to provide help and information when physical offices were closed due to lockdowns.

## Method or approach

The researchers used **Structural Topic Modeling (STM)**. This is a computer-assisted way to find themes in a massive amount of text (over 260,000 posts) while also looking at factors like poverty rates and whether a town is urban or rural.

## Case, country, platform, and period

Country / region: Chile (Santiago Metropolitan Region)
Election or political context: COVID-19 pandemic and the lead-up to the 2021 municipal elections, following the 2019 social uprising
Platform: Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter
Time period: March 3, 2020, to June 28, 2021
Type of material studied: 264,387 organic social media posts

## Main relevance for Diego’s paper

- **Platformization (G3):** It proves that platforms aren't just for ads; they act as "active infrastructure" or digital versions of government offices in unequal societies.
- **Chile Context (G4):** It explains why mayors were the most trusted political figures in Chile after the 2019 protests caused a loss of trust in national leaders.
- **Method (G2):** It serves as a strong precedent for using STM to analyze a large number of Spanish-language posts.

## Useful claims

- Socioeconomic conditions (like poverty) drive digital strategies more than political ones (like which party the mayor belongs to).
- During lockdowns, mayors in poor towns drastically increased social media use to manage welfare and aid programs.
- Different platforms have different "jobs": Twitter was used for health alerts, while Facebook was used for social support and registries.
- Digital tools don't always "democratize" politics; sometimes they just reflect and absorb existing inequalities from the real world.
