# Source Note — ARNAUDO 2017 COMPUTATIONAL PROPAGANDA BRAZIL

## Basic information

Author: Dan Arnaudo
Year: 2017
Title: Computational Propaganda in Brazil: Social Bots during Elections
Journal / book / source: Project on Computational Propaganda (Working Paper No. 2017.8), University of Oxford
PDF filename: PDF004
Source ID: PDF004

## What this source is about

This study looks at "computational propaganda" in Brazil, which includes using automated bots, "cyborg" accounts, and fake news to sway public opinion. A key finding is the "Permanent Campaign"—the idea that digital tools (like botnets and WhatsApp groups) created for an election aren't turned off afterward; they are reused to fuel protests and political crises, like the 2016 impeachment.

## Method or approach

Mixed-methods: The author combined qualitative interviews with experts and journalists with a quantitative analysis of Twitter data and hashtags. The study also used "Bot or Not" tools to identify automated accounts.

## Case, country, platform, and period

Country / region: Brazil
Election or political context: 2014 Presidential Election, 2016 Rio municipal elections, and the 2016 impeachment of Dilma Rousseff.
Platform: Twitter, Facebook, and WhatsApp.
Time period: 2014–2017.
Type of material studied: Twitter data (over 300,000 tweets) and expert interviews.

## Main relevance for Diego’s paper

This is a foundational regional case for **Platformization (G3)** and **LatAm Context (G4)**. It describes how campaigns use the interplay between "private" networks (WhatsApp) and "public" feeds (Facebook/Twitter) to mobilize people in volatile democracies. It serves as a comparative baseline for understanding later cycles in Chile (like 2019-2021).

## Useful claims

- Digital campaign machinery is often repurposed for protests rather than being dismantled (the "Permanent Campaign").
- Campaigns use "cyborgs"—real people who "donate" their profiles to campaign software—to bypass bot-detection systems.
- Closed networks like WhatsApp are used as "dark spaces" to seed messages that are later pushed out to public forums.
- Algorithms are treated as "active infrastructures" that campaigns explicitly try to game or manipulate.
- There is a high risk of "algorithmic manipulation" and misinformation in young democracies with fragmented party systems.
