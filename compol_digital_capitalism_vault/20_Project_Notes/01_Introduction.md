# Introduction

Online political microtargeting is, by operational design, a silent practice (Agan, 2007). Each user receives a different message; no shared public sphere registers what was said, to whom, with what budget, or under whose authorship. The Cambridge Analytica disclosures of 2018 made this opacity politically intolerable in the United States and the United Kingdom and triggered a now-substantial regulatory and analytical response, but that response has remained largely confined to the North Atlantic (Richardson, Witzleb, & Paterson, 2020; Witzleb, 2021). The empirical literature on microtargeting beyond that perimeter is sparse, and what little exists in Latin America has approached the phenomenon from political-science angles rather than from direct examination of advertising content and audience composition (Tejada, 2016). The Chilean 2021 presidential race — fought across two rounds, seven candidates, a fragmented party system, and the political tail of the 2019 social uprising — produced one of the densest digital propaganda corpora in recent regional electoral history, and it sits almost entirely outside the comparative empirical record.

The visibility of that corpus is itself a recent and uneven achievement. Following the Cambridge Analytica scandal, Facebook, Google, and Twitter were pressured into creating publicly accessible ad libraries that disclose paid political advertising, the entities that funded it, and partial information on the demographic audiences each ad reached (Vrancken, 2022). These repositories are the precondition for any empirical study of microtargeting at scale: without them, the practice is observable only through leaks, whistleblowers, or platform cooperation. They are also incomplete. The targeting parameters disclosed in the Facebook Ad Library are a strict subset of those available to advertisers through the platform's media-buying interface, which means that even the best public dataset documents what the campaign chose to do at a coarser resolution than what the campaign was technically able to do. The empirical work that follows operates inside this gap and is conditioned by it.

Microtargeting itself is a recent enough object that the literature has not yet stabilized a single definition. Borgesius et al. (2018) and Gorton (2016) characterize it as the construction of refined messages segmented and delivered to narrow categories of voters on the basis of demographic, behavioral, and preference data harvested from the voters themselves. López Ortega (2021) emphasizes the underlying technique: online segmentation grounded in individual-level data with the objective of producing a political result. What separates microtargeting from the political marketing that preceded it (Maarek, 2014) is not the use of segmented messaging — campaigns have always done that — but the granularity at which segmentation operates and the data infrastructure that supports it. The operative claim in the literature is that this granularity reaches surgical precision: distinct messages aimed at audiences sometimes numbering in the low thousands, calibrated to specific psychometric or behavioral profiles. That claim has not been systematically tested against the public ad libraries of any country in the region, and the empirical evidence assembled here suggests that the actual targeting precision practiced by Chilean campaigns in 2021 was substantially looser than the literature's headline cases would predict — a finding whose theoretical implications run beyond the Chilean case.

The Chilean case is not incidental. Between October 2019 and December 2021, Chile passed through a social uprising of historic scale, a pandemic-induced collapse of in-person campaigning, a national plebiscite that opened a constitutional process demanded from the streets, and the dislocation of the two political blocs — center-left Concertación-heir and center-right Alianza-heir — that had administered the post-1990 transition (Garretón, Joignant, Somma, & Campos, 2016; Luna & Rosenblatt, 2012). The dislocation was visible in the candidate field itself: Gabriel Boric, a deputy from a small left coalition rooted in the student movement, displaced the Communist Party's Daniel Jadue in the primary of Apruebo Dignidad; José Antonio Kast, founder of a party split from the historic right, displaced the official-coalition candidate Sebastián Sichel in the first round; and Franco Parisi, polling third without setting foot in the country and without buying a single Facebook ad, ran a campaign whose digital infrastructure was built almost entirely on data-driven voter profiling. In this configuration, the 2021 election was less a routine succession than a stress test of how a destabilized party system transmits, segments, and targets political messaging through digital infrastructure that itself imported its operating logic from US and European campaign technology (Visconti, 2021).

A second feature of the Chilean conjuncture sharpens the analytical interest. Pandemic restrictions during 2020 and 2021 collapsed the traditional offline campaign — door-knocking, public rallies, street propaganda — into the digital channel almost entirely, accelerating a trend that had been advancing since the irruption of Marco Enríquez-Ominami's outsider candidacy in 2009 and that had been reinforced through the seven successive elections held between the 2020 constitutional plebiscite and the 2021 presidential runoff. By the time of the legal campaign window, what had been a supplementary strategic terrain in earlier cycles had become the principal one, and the campaign teams that managed it operated under regulatory constraints designed for an earlier media environment: Ley 19.628 of 1999 governs the use of personal data, was drafted before the consolidation of platform capitalism, and contains the structural gaps that allowed firms such as Instagis to build voter-profiling services for Chilean campaigns from 2013 onward with modest scrutiny (CIPER, 2018). The combination of accelerated digital migration, weak data-protection law, and acute political dislocation makes Chile 2021 a particularly informative case for examining what data-driven campaigning actually looks like outside the small set of cases that dominate the comparative literature.

Two analytical questions organize the empirical work that follows. First, what is the latent thematic structure of paid political advertising in the 2021 Chilean campaign, across candidates, blocs, and rounds? Second, given that structure, do campaigns deliver different topics to different demographic publics, and if so, with what precision and with what concentration? The first question is approached through Latent Dirichlet Allocation (Blei, Ng, & Jordan, 2003), estimated independently for each candidate-round and bloc-round subset of the corpus, with topic content interpreted post hoc through inspection of the highest-probability words and the candidate's documented programmatic positions. The second question is approached by computing, for each topic, the conditional demographic distribution of its ads relative to a campaign-wide baseline. Three indices formalize the comparison: a Relative Exposure Index that measures, segment by segment, whether topic delivery exceeds or falls short of the baseline; a Topic Targeting Divergence based on Jensen-Shannon distance that ranks topics by how distinctively their audiences differ from the baseline; and a Targeting Gini Coefficient that captures the concentration of each topic's reach across demographic cells. The first index operationalizes targeting at the cell level. The second and third, taken jointly, produce a structural typology distinguishing surgically targeted topics from broadcast ones, and topics whose audiences are concentrated from those whose reach is dispersed.

The methodological framing is text-as-data (Benoit, 2020), and it carries a specific commitment. Topic modeling extracts a thematic skeleton from the campaign without imposing a priori categories drawn from the analyst's prior expectations about what the candidates "should" have said. The demographic segmentation analysis then asks whether that skeleton, once recovered, was directed at specific audiences in ways that would not arise from random allocation of content across the full ad corpus. This sequence — content first, then audience structure — places the burden of proof on the data, not on the interpretation. It also clarifies what microtargeting can and cannot be claimed to be from this evidence: a pattern of differential delivery of distinct topics to distinct demographic cells, observable at the level of audience composition, but not directly observable at the level of individual psychometric profiling, which the Facebook Ad Library does not disclose.

The empirical findings cut against the literature's strongest version of the microtargeting claim. Topic-by-segment delivery in 2021 Chile was differentiated, but the differentiation operated at the scale of broad demographic categories — gender, age tranches of roughly a decade, region — rather than the narrow psychographic niches that the Cambridge Analytica disclosures had made paradigmatic. Many ads were delivered to multiple targeting decisions simultaneously, with several of those decisions carrying very small relative weights (under one percent), suggesting that part of what looks like microtargeting is, in operational terms, audience testing prior to a more concentrated delivery decision. The candidates differed sharply, however, in the targeting precision of their content portfolios. Right-bloc campaigns, particularly Kast's, ran a smaller number of high-divergence topics aimed at concentrated demographic audiences; left-bloc campaigns, particularly Boric's, ran a broader thematic portfolio with comparatively flatter audience concentration. The runoff between Boric and Kast then produced a partial reorganization of these strategies, with topic births and deaths between rounds that map cleanly onto the strategic problem each campaign faced after the first-round result. These contrasts are the empirical core of the paper, and the formal indices are the instrument that makes them legible.

The argumentative stake reaches beyond the case. The political-communication literature has been organized around the question of whether data-driven campaigning marks a qualitative break in democratic politics — a new stage of personalization (Adam & Maier, 2010; Kruikemeier, Lecheler, & Metz, 2020), a new vector of polarization and manipulation (Borgesius et al., 2018; Gorton, 2016), or a new form of platform-mediated representation (Witzleb & Paterson, 2021). The literature's framing of that question has been shaped by a small number of high-resolution cases drawn mostly from consolidated democracies with well-developed data-protection regimes. Chile 2021 is neither of those, and it offers what the comparative literature has lacked: an empirical baseline on what data-driven campaigning looks like when it is practiced under regulatory and institutional conditions closer to those of much of the Global South. The structural typology of microtargeting strategies that emerges from this evidence — surgical, strategically differentiated, concentrated-but-not-distinctive, broadcast — is one analytical contribution. The downward revision of the literature's surgical-precision claim, in light of how campaigns actually used the Facebook ad infrastructure under the conditions of public disclosure, is the other.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 sets the political and technological context: the post-2019 Chilean conjuncture, the trajectory of data-driven campaigning from Cambridge Analytica forward, and the local infrastructure (Instagis, the regulatory vacuum) that made Chile a receptive environment for these techniques. Section 3 reviews the literature on microtargeting, personalization, and computational political communication, situating the present contribution within the empirical and methodological gaps that motivate it. Section 4 develops the analytical framework, including the formal specification of the three indices. Section 5 describes the data and the preprocessing pipeline. Section 6 presents the results, organized by round, bloc, and candidate. Section 7 discusses implications for the political-economy and strategic-communication literatures. Section 8 concludes.
