## Section 2.3: Integrated Theoretical Framework and Empirical Positioning

**Reconceptualizing Microtargeting within Platform Capitalism**
_This paragraph redefines microtargeting from a neutral campaign tool to an active mechanism of platform capitalist accumulation and data commodification, grounding the analysis in Christian Fuchs' political economy perspective._
> The study of digital political communication must look beyond the surface of campaign messaging to examine the structural power dynamics of digital capitalism. As Fuchs (2019) argues, platform capitalism operates by exploiting user labor and extracting data to commodify user attention. Under this critical lens, online microtargeting is not merely a tactical tool for political persuasion, but a practice deeply embedded in the logic of capital accumulation. Digital platforms commodify political communication itself, turning voter attention and personal data into proprietary assets (Dean, 2019). By using Facebook's advertising infrastructure, campaigns participate in and reinforce this extraction process, operating within an environment characterized by severe power asymmetries where corporate platforms govern the visibility of political discourse (Fuchs, 2019). Therefore, a critical synthesis must examine microtargeting as a manifestation of platform power rather than a neutral channel of democratic participation.

**Demystifying the Granularity of Targeted Political Advertising**
_This paragraph contrasts the dystopian narratives of psychographic voter manipulation with the empirical realities of broad demographic targeting in campaign practices._
> A major debate in the literature on digital campaigning centers on the granularity and effectiveness of online targeting. A critical, often alarmist line of research conceptualizes microtargeting as a highly sophisticated mechanism of behavioral manipulation that utilizes psychographic data to deliver fragmented, personalized messages to narrow niches (Gorton, 2016). Conversely, empirical assessments suggest that microtargeting is constrained by campaign budgets and platform architectures, functioning primarily as a pragmatic "emphasis" tool rather than a tool of mass deception (Baldwin-Philippi, 2018; Kreiss, 2016). Indeed, research on paid digital ads shows that campaigns frequently rely on broad demographic targeting rather than hyper-personalized segments (Anstead et al., 2025). This tension highlights a key methodological blind spot in the field: an over-focus on the content of the ads at the expense of investigating the platform infrastructures that determine how targeting is actually operationalized and distributed.

**Epistemic Asymmetries and Platform Governance: The "Scandalous Design"**
_This paragraph operationalizes the role of platforms as active political actors that control data transparency and researcher access in response to public scandals._
> Platforms are not passive conduits for campaign messages, but active political intermediaries that shape the conditions of democratic publicity. Through what Bossetta (2020) terms "scandalous design," social media platforms continually alter their architectures, API accesses, and protocols in response to public controversies to shield themselves from regulation. These design changes restrict data transparency, creating severe epistemic asymmetries for journalists and researchers attempting to audit political advertising (Leerssen et al., 2023). While tools like the Facebook Ad Library provide a public record of ads, they are limited by incomplete metadata, restrictive searches, and algorithmic opacity (Leerssen et al., 2023). Thus, platform power is exercised not only through algorithmic sorting (Thorson et al., 2019) but also through the governance of access to information, which limits public oversight of targeting practices.

**Provincializing the Digital: The Limits of Global North Campaign Models**
_This paragraph identifies the theoretical and empirical limitations of applying digital campaign models developed in the Global North to Latin American political contexts._
> The theoretical baseline of digital political communication is overwhelmingly derived from studies of the United States and Western Europe, creating a major gap in transferability to the Global South (Bimber, 2014; Hanson et al., 2010). When applied to Latin America, these models often fail to account for structural fragmentation, high digital penetration, and distinct regional campaign dynamics (Tejada, 2016). In Latin American contexts, platforms operate within volatile party systems and deep socio-political crises, where computational propaganda and digital astroturfing have unique local characteristics (Arnaudo, 2017; Chagas, 2022). It is therefore necessary to provincialize the dominant theories of digital campaigning and situate them within the specific institutional, social, and economic realities of the region.

**Chile's Post-Estallido Crisis as a Constitutive Campaign Environment**
_This paragraph positions the 2021 Chilean presidential election as a constitutive context of institutional legitimacy crisis and political reorganization post-social uprising._
> The 2021 Chilean presidential election cannot be understood through generic models of digital campaigning; rather, it was constitutively shaped by the country's institutional crisis. Following the 2019 social uprising (*estallido social*) and the subsequent constitutional process, the traditional party system experienced a severe decline in legitimacy, leading to structural fragmentation (Argote et al., 2025). This bloc dislocation displaced the traditional center-left and center-right coalitions, allowing anti-establishment and peripheral candidates—such as Gabriel Boric, José Antonio Kast, and Franco Parisi—to rise to prominence. In this highly polarized and fragmented environment, digital platforms became essential campaign infrastructures for candidates seeking to bypass traditional media systems and construct direct connections with a distrustful electorate (Luna et al., 2022).

**Pandemic Restrictions and the Accelerated Shift to Platformized Infrastructure**
_This paragraph explains how the physical constraints of the COVID-19 pandemic intersected with the political crisis to force campaign communication onto digital platforms._
> The material constraints of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2021 acted as a catalyst that accelerated the transfer of campaign activities to digital spaces. With physical campaigning restricted, political parties were forced to adapt their communication strategies to platform architectures. This reliance on social media meant that the distribution of political messages was subordinated to the algorithmic logic of platform capitalism (Fuchs, 2019). The volume of ads generated during the campaign—concentrated during periods of high political intensity—reflects this structural shift, as campaigns utilized Facebook's tools as their primary medium of voter contact.

**The Rise of Platform-Centric Candidates and Coalition Dislocation**
_This paragraph links the decline of traditional political party organizations to the emergence of platform-centric candidacies in the 2021 Chilean election._
> The displacement of traditional party structures in Chile led to the emergence of highly personalized, platform-centric candidacies. Candidates who lacked the backing of established party machines, such as Franco Parisi campaigning entirely from the United States, leveraged digital media to construct political viability. This shift aligns with the transition toward what Gerbaudo (2019) terms the "platform party," where political organization is mediated by digital interfaces. By targeting specific demographics through digital platforms, candidates bypassed traditional mass media gatekeepers, utilizing social media to build direct support networks (Álvarez-Fuentes et al., 2024).

**Empirical Realities of Chilean Microtargeting: Broad Broadcasting vs. Granular Niches**
_This paragraph presents the empirical findings showing that microtargeting in Chile 2021 was pragmatic and hybrid, combining broadcast and targeted elements._
> The empirical analysis of Facebook ads metadata from the 2021 Chilean presidential campaign challenges alarmist assertions of hyper-granular microtargeting. While candidate campaigns engaged in demographic segmentation based on age, gender, and region, their targeting strategies remained hybrid and pragmatic, often combining targeted ads with broad, broadcast-style messaging (Baldwin-Philippi, 2018). This findings confirm that in practice, online targeting is limited by budgetary and structural constraints, rather than being an omnipotent tool of manipulation (Kreiss, 2016). Therefore, rather than a total fragmentation of the public sphere, digital campaigning in Chile operated as a form of targeted emphasis, aiming to increase the salience of issues rather than manipulate individual voter psychologies (Vrielink et al., 2025).

**Ideological Asymmetries and Thematic Coherence in Political Ad Output**
_This paragraph analyzes the ideological differences and thematic coherence in the candidates' ad outputs using topic modeling._
> The application of Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic modeling to the Facebook ad campaigns reveals significant ideological asymmetries in thematic output. The campaign of Gabriel Boric on the left displayed a high level of thematic coherence, focusing consistently on social rights such as health, pensions, education, and sustainability (Álvarez-Fuentes et al., 2024). In contrast, the right-wing campaigns showed lower thematic coherence, reflecting the ideological fragmentation and distance between candidates like Sebastián Sichel and José Antonio Kast. These findings indicate that digital campaigns do not create ideological divisions, but rather amplify pre-existing political dynamics and institutional fragmentations.

**Separating Democratic Anxiety from Empirical Manipulation**
_This paragraph refutes dystopian narratives of deceptive microtargeting by showing that Chilean candidates used targeting for emphasis rather than fundamental contradiction._
> Finally, the empirical analysis of content and targeting congruence in Chile 2021 refutes fears that microtargeting is used to deliver contradictory messages to deceive different voter groups. Candidates maintained highly stable political messaging across different demographic categories; for instance, Boric consistently advocated for social reforms, while Kast focused on security and family values across all targeted ads. This indicates that microtargeting in Chile operated under a logic of targeted emphasis—highlighting different aspects of a candidate's platform to relevant groups—rather than a logic of fundamental deception (Kreiss, 2017). The democratic anxieties surrounding microtargeting therefore reflect broader concerns about the legitimacy of democratic institutions and the rise of populism, rather than documented evidence of behavioral manipulation.
