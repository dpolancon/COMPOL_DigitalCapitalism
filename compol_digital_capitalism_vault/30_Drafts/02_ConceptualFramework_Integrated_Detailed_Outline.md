- [[CF01_Microtargeting_As_Digital_Capitalism]] (from `conceptual_framework_notes/CF01_Microtargeting_As_Digital_Capitalism.md`):
    - *Theoretical Position*: From a critical theoretical perspective, grounded in Christian Fuchs' conception of digital capitalism, microtargeting practices in political campaigns instantiate the logic of platform capitalism. This perspective posits that digital capitalism is characterized by the exploitation of users' labor and data, with platforms acting as instruments of capitalist accumulation. Microtargeting, as a key component of digital political advertising, embodies this logic by leveraging user data to create highly targeted and personalized advertisements. This not only reflects the commodification of attention and the exploitation of user labor but also underscores the power asymmetries inherent in digital political infrastructure, where platforms wield significant control over the dissemination of information and the shaping of political discourse. By adopting Fuchs' digital capitalism lens, we critically examine how microtargeting practices reinforce the dominance of platform capitalism, perpetuate the exploitation of users, and influence the political economy of digital campaigns.
    - *Empirical Implications*: The framework of microtargeting as digital capitalism offers significant empirical implications for the analysis of Gabriel Boric's and other candidates' Facebook ads in Chile 2021. By applying Fuchs' digital capitalism lens, researchers can critically examine how microtargeting practices were used to exploit user data, commodify attention, and shape political discourse during the campaign. This involves analyzing the targeting mechanisms, ad visibility, and audience control strategies employed by candidates, as well as the role of Facebook's algorithmic inference systems in categorizing users and influencing the visibility of political content. Furthermore, the study can investigate how microtargeting practices reinforced or challenged the power asymmetries inherent in digital political infrastructure, including the dominance of platform capitalism and the exploitation of user labor. By doing so, the empirical analysis can provide insights into the political economy of digital campaigns in Chile 2021, highlighting the ways in which microtargeting practices instantiate the logic of platform capitalism and shape the contours of political communication in the digital age.
- [[PDF_002_ANSTEAD_ETAL_2025]] (from `papers/PDF_002_ANSTEAD_ETAL_2025.md`):
    - *Bibliographic Identity*: **Facebook election advertising: dangerous for democracy or politics as usual? The case of the 2017 UK general election.** by Nick Anstead, João C. Magalhães, Richard Stupart & Damian Tambini. (Journal of Information Technology & Politics (Taylor & Francis).)
    - *Core Claims*:
      *   While broad demographic targeting occurs, the **timing of the election campaign** (point in cycle) is the single most important factor in what appears in a user's feed, outweighing specific targeting variables.
      *   There is **limited evidence of geographical targeting** (surprising given the UK's First-Past-The-Post system).
      *   Facebook advertising largely resembles "politics as usual" in terms of negativity and topic range compared to traditional channels in this specific context, rather than representing a radical disruption.

- [[NB01_Paid_Digital_Ads]] (from `cluster_notes/NB01_Paid_Digital_Ads.md`):
    - *Synthesis of Sources*: The study of paid digital ads reveals a complex landscape of targeting mechanisms, ad visibility, and audience control. Anstead et al. (2025) found that paid Facebook election advertising during the 2017 UK General Election was characterized by broad demographic targeting, with limited evidence of geographical targeting. The study used a dataset created by the civil society organization Who Targets Me, which involved a browser plugin installed by over 11,000 volunteers to harvest Facebook adverts encountered during browsing. The authors argued that while microtargeting does not automatically equate to democratic disruption, the lack of transparency in ad targeting and the potential for algorithmic misdirection are concerns that need to be addressed.
  
  Bossetta (2020) introduced the concept of "Scandalous Design," where social media platforms alter their architecture and protocols in response to public scandals, positioning themselves as political actors rather than neutral channels. The study highlighted the importance of understanding platform design changes as a mechanism of political influence, particularly in the context of paid advertising. Bossetta argued that platforms use design changes to mitigate the consequences of scandals and generate goodwill with governments, while maintaining control over data transparency.
  
  Dobber et al. (2020) empirically demonstrated that self-produced political deepfakes can significantly lower attitudes toward a depicted politician, with microtargeting techniques capable of amplifying this effect. The study used a laboratory experiment to simulate the effect of microtargeting on deepfake content and found that participants who were exposed to microtargeted deepfakes were more likely to report negative attitudes toward the politician.
  
  Hameleers et al. (2020) studied the elements of online populist communication that trigger user engagement on social media. The study found that stylistic elements, such as emotionality and first-person references, were more effective in triggering engagement than ideological content. The authors argued that this style-over-content approach can contribute to the spread of populist ideology and strengthen polarized divides.
  
  Leerssen et al. (2023) examined the practical usage and impact of the Facebook Ad Library by journalists as a tool for public accountability and watchdog journalism. The study found that the Ad Library creates a public record of personalized ads, but has significant technical and design limitations, including incomplete metadata and restricted search functionality.
  
  Thorson et al. (2019) investigated the intersection of user behavior and platform algorithms, specifically how Facebook's algorithmic inference systems categorize users based on political interest and influence the visibility of political and news content in the user's News Feed. The study found that algorithmic inference creates consequences for exposure, with users categorized as interested in news/politics more likely to see such content in their feeds.
  
  Vrielink et al. (2025) studied the influence of targeted online political advertising on voters' perceived issue importance during the 2022 US Midterm and 2021 Dutch General elections. The study found that exposure to online ads increases the salience of issues among voters, but there is no evidence that issue-based targeting successfully aligns ads with voters' pre-existing issue preferences.
  
  In terms of ad visibility, disclosure, and audience control, the studies reveal a mixed picture. Anstead et al. (2025) found that ad visibility is non-transparent, with ads appearing in the private space of an individual's Facebook feed. Bossetta (2020) argued that platforms control access to data via API restrictions and search filters, limiting the ability of users and researchers to understand targeting mechanisms. Dobber et al. (2020) found that microtargeting can amplify the effect of deepfake content, but the study did not examine the visibility or disclosure of such content. Hameleers et al. (2020) studied organic posts, which are publicly visible, but the study did not examine paid ads or their visibility. Leerssen et al. (2023) found that the Facebook Ad Library provides a public record of personalized ads, but with limited visibility and disclosure. Thorson et al. (2019) argued that algorithmic inference creates consequences for exposure, with users having limited control over the content they see in their News Feed. Vrielink et al. (2025) found that dynamic media usage allows for specific placement of targeted ads, but the study did not examine the visibility or disclosure of such ads.
  
  Overall, the studies suggest that paid digital ads are a complex and multifaceted phenomenon, with targeting mechanisms, ad visibility, and audience control playing important roles in shaping political communication and influence. However, the lack of transparency and disclosure in ad targeting, as well as the potential for algorithmic misdirection, are concerns that need to be addressed.
    - *Gaps and Tensions*: The literature on paid digital ads reveals several gaps and tensions. One gap is the lack of transparency and disclosure in ad targeting, which limits the ability of users and researchers to understand the mechanisms of influence. Another gap is the limited understanding of the long-term effects of microtargeting on political attitudes and behaviors. There is also a tension between the potential benefits of targeted advertising, such as increased issue salience, and the potential risks, such as the spread of misinformation and the amplification of deepfake content. Additionally, there is a tension between the role of platforms as neutral channels and their role as political actors, with implications for the governance of online political advertising. Overall, the literature highlights the need for further research on the complex and multifaceted phenomenon of paid digital ads, with a focus on addressing the gaps and tensions identified in this synthesis.
**Paragraph Title: The North American Baseline: Psychographics and the Dystopian Lens**
> The study of digital political communication in the Global North has largely coalesced around a definition of microtargeting centered on psychographic personalization and extreme audience fragmentation. As conceptualized by Anstead et al. (2025) and Vrielink et al. (2025), digital political advertising is theorized as leveraging granular voter profiles to deliver highly targeted messages tailored to individual psychological vulnerabilities. This conceptualization has fueled a dystopian anxiety within democratic theory, positioning microtargeting as an omnipotent mechanism of behavioral manipulation that threatens to dissolve the shared public sphere. However, this preoccupation with the persuasive content of individual ad copies creates a methodological blind spot. To evaluate whether these concerns hold empirical weight, it is necessary to transition from studying ad text in isolation to auditing the structural distribution of ad metadata. By applying a systematic analysis of Facebook's target audience metadata (NB01_Paid_Digital_Ads), researchers can map the actual granularity of campaign reach. This method provides the capacity to test whether campaigns are truly targeting narrow demographic niches or simply employing digital platforms for disguised broadcasting, thereby grounding theoretical fears in material evidence.

**Paragraph Title: The Epistemic Gap: Platform Infrastructure and the Black Box**
> Despite the dominance of the dystopian manipulation model, empirical researchers argue that microtargeting is heavily constrained by organizational budgets, platform algorithms, and political realities, functioning more as a tool for targeted emphasis than deception (PDF_002_ANSTEAD_ETAL_2025). Yet, a significant epistemic gap remains: by focusing almost exclusively on ad copy, political communication literature treats platforms as neutral channels and leaves the actual mechanism of targeting as a "black box" (CF02_Methodological_Approach_Topic_Modeling). This oversight ignores how platforms operate as active political actors that shape visibility and extract data for capital accumulation. To bridge this gap, this research proposes a methodological shift from message-centric analyses to metadata audits (NB01_Paid_Digital_Ads). By analyzing the backend targeting metadata—including age, region, and gender segmentation—this approach possesses the capacity to expose the platformized campaign infrastructure. Rather than merely documenting the rhetoric candidates project, this method enables the systematic evaluation of how platform structures and candidate strategies interact to distribute visibility, thereby making the platform's proprietary targeting mechanism legible to public scrutiny (PDF_026_LEERSSEN_ETAL_2023).

**Paragraph Title: The Transferability Problem: Situating Digital Politics in Chile**
> Applying campaign models developed in the Global North directly to the Latin American context introduces a serious transferability problem. In Chile, while scholarly work on digital political communication exists, it has historically focused on general social network usage and remained disconnected from the specific structural dynamics of online microtargeting (PDF_029_LUNA_ETAL_2022_CHILE). This disconnection is particularly problematic given that the 2021 Chilean elections occurred in a highly volatile, post-uprising (*estallido social*) context of deep institutional crisis and constitutional rewrite (CF05_Chile_2021_As_Constitutive_Context). Unlike the stable party systems assumed in North American literature, the Chilean case represents a moment of structural dislocation where traditional coalitions fractured and anti-establishment campaigns emerged. To resolve this transferability gap, we employ Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic modeling (CF02_Methodological_Approach_Topic_Modeling). By applying LDA, this research is uniquely equipped to discover the latent thematic topics within candidate ads and trace how they map onto different demographic targeting segments. This computational approach allows us to analyze how digital campaign strategies are situated within Chile’s crisis context, offering a situated theory of platform-mediated political communication in the Global South rather than importing foreign conceptual frameworks.

**Paragraph Title: Assessing Manipulation: Message Congruence and Targeted Emphasis**
> The normative debate surrounding the ethics of microtargeting centers on the potential for campaigns to engage in voter manipulation and democratic deception. Under the critical perspective, microtargeting is theorized as an opaque instrument of behavioral manipulation that enables candidates to send contradictory promises to different groups, leading to a fragmented and deceptive public sphere (PDF_013_DOBBER_ETAL_2020). Conversely, more optimistic accounts suggest that targeting acts as a tool for targeted emphasis, aligning campaign messages with pre-existing voter issue interests without necessarily resorting to deceit (PDF_002_ANSTEAD_ETAL_2025). To resolve this fundamental debate (CF04_Democratic_Risk_Vs_Evidence), this research proposes a methodology of message congruence testing (NB02_Topic_Modeling_Methods). By cross-tabulating the latent thematic topics identified through LDA with the targeting metadata, this method provides the capacity to evaluate the consistency of candidate messaging across different targeted demographic audiences. Specifically, it allows the research to determine whether candidate messaging was contradictory across age or regional boundaries, or whether campaigns maintained a stable core message while shifting emphasis, thereby providing an empirical test to distinguish between a logic of targeted emphasis and a logic of fundamental manipulation.
