### 1. Bibliographic & Content Extraction

**Bibliographic Identity**
*   **Title:** Tweeting Antagonism: (De)Polarizing Rhetoric and Tone in Colombia’s 2022 Presidential Campaign
*   **Authors:** Laura Gamboa, Sandra Botero, Lisa Zanotti
*   **Journal:** *Latin American Politics and Society* (Cambridge University Press)
*   **Publication Year:** 2024
*   **DOI:** 10.1017/lap.2024.14
*   **Author Affiliation:** Lisa Zanotti (Universidad Diego Portales, Santiago de Chile) – *Crucial link to Diego context.*

**What the Source Studies**
*   **Topic:** The relationship between political rhetoric (polarizing vs. depolarizing) and emotional tone (negative vs. positive) on Twitter during a presidential election.
*   **Focus:** Measuring "supply side" of rhetoric (candidates' tweets) and its effect on "user engagement" (likes/retweets).

**Method and Corpus**
*   **Method:** Quantitative analysis of Twitter data (now X), analyzing word choice (tone) and rhetorical coding (polarizing/depolarizing).
*   **Corpus:** Tweets from the four leading candidates in Colombia’s 2022 race: Gustavo Petro, Rodolfo Hernández, Federico Gutiérrez, and Sergio Fajardo.
*   **Metrics:** Engagement rates (likes, retweets) compared against message content.

**Core Claims**
*   **Tone > Rhetoric:** Negative tone generates more engagement than polarizing rhetoric, though the effect varies by candidate.
*   **Depolarization Works:** Depolarizing rhetoric generated greater engagement than polarizing rhetoric for some candidates (Petro, Hernández, Gutiérrez), challenging the "polarization always wins" norm.
*   **Candidate Type Matters:** Anti-establishment candidates (Petro) did better with depolarizing/positive tone; establishment candidates (Fajardo) benefited more from polarizing rhetoric.
*   **Hope for Democracy:** Positive, uniting messages can increase engagement under certain conditions, countering the doom narrative of social media polarization.

### 2. Contextual Extraction (Chile/LatAm Focus)

**Country/Election Studied**
*   **Country:** Colombia
*   **Election:** 2022 Presidential Campaign.
*   **Note:** The text does *not* study Chile 2019, the Constitutional Process, or Chile 2021 directly. However, it positions itself as a LatAm comparative case to fill the literature gap regarding "less-institutionalized parties."

**LatAm Distinctiveness**
*   **Party System:** The authors explicitly contrast their findings with US/Europe studies, noting that Colombia has "less-institutionalized parties," "unstable party brands," and "strong personalistic dynamics."
*   **Relevance to Chile:** This distinction is highly relevant to Chile 2021, where the party system was also characterized by fragmentation and personal brands (e.g., Bachelet, Kast, Boric) rather than rigid party machines.

**Platforms Used**
*   **Platform:** Twitter (now X).
*   **Function:** Described as a "powerful political tool" for communication without intermediaries.

**Contrast Case for Chile 2021**
*   **Direct Mention:** None.
*   **Theoretical Contrast:** The paper argues that because Colombian politics feature "individual rather than party reputations" (Page 134), this is a valid theoretical parallel for Chile 2021, which also saw a surge of individualist candidacies post-2019.

**Justification Sentence for Chile as Theoretical Case**
*   *Note: The text justifies the **Colombian** case, not Chile. However, the sentence that justifies the **LatAm** case is:*
    > "Most studies of polarizing rhetoric in Twitter have focused on the United States and Europe... This study aims to uncover how polarizing rhetoric works in a context where party brands are unstable and thus less meaningful... Like many Latin American countries, Colombian politics are characterized by strong personalistic dynamics." (Page 134)
*   **Application to Chile:** This sentence justifies using Colombia (and by extension Chile) as a theoretical case because both share the "unstable party brands" characteristic absent in the US/EU literature.

### 3. Digitalization & Platformization Analysis

**Concept of Digitalization/Platformization**
*   **Definition:** The source treats digitalization primarily as **communication tools** and **rhetorical channels**.
*   **Platformization:** Framed through the lens of **algorithmic architecture** (Page 133) which "aggravates" polarizing rhetoric by creating echo chambers and favoring high-engagement content.

**Actors**
*   **Suppliers:** Presidential candidates (Petro, Hernández, Gutiérrez, Fajardo).
*   **Consumers:** Social media users (general public).
*   **Intermediaries:** Twitter/X algorithms, traditional media (mentioned as attracted by Twitter activity).

**Platform Framing (Channel vs. Infrastructure)**
*   **Framing:** Primarily as a **Channel**. The authors view Twitter as a tool "to communicate with their constituencies without intermediaries" (Page 132).
*   **Infrastructure:** It is also framed as an **Infrastructure** regarding its "aggregative functionalities and algorithmic architecture" (Page 133) that shapes visibility and engagement regardless of content intent.

**Geographic Scope**
*   **Specific:** Colombia.
*   **Scope:** Local case study intended to inform broader LatAm theory.

**Mechanisms Linking Platforms to Campaign Strategy**
1.  **Engagement Economy:** Candidates use negative tone/polarizing rhetoric to "gain followers" and "attract the attention of traditional media" (Page 136).
2.  **Algorithmic Visibility:** The platform favors content that generates interaction (negative/controversial), incentivizing antagonism.
3.  **Personal Branding:** Platforms allow politicians to build "individual rather than party reputations" (Page 134).
