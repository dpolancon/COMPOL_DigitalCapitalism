### Bibliographic Identity
*   **Source Study:** *Unveiling Disinformation: Mapping Attacks on Brazil’s Electoral System and the Response of the Superior Electoral Court (2018–2023)*.
*   **Method:** Mixed methods. Quantitative content analysis (420 verification articles from TSE's *Fato ou Boato* website) and Qualitative analysis (6 in-depth expert interviews with TSE members, official documents, reports).
*   **Corpus:** 420 verification articles (2018–2023), TSE official documents, promotional materials, expert interviews.
*   **Core Claims:** Disinformation attacks against Brazil's electoral system surged (TSE targets 15% in 2018 to 27% in 2022); falsehoods focus on electronic voting system vulnerability (25%) and TSE illegal actions (15%). The TSE employs a unique, non-regulatory, multisectoral strategy involving independent fact-checkers and platform collaboration (e.g., zero-rating access).
*   **Relevance to Diego's paper:** (Assuming Diego's paper concerns electoral integrity, disinformation, or LatAm political communication) This source serves as a **precedent** for institutional responses to disinformation. It provides empirical data on how a judicial body (TSE) acts as a counterweight to populist disinformation, offering a comparative model for Diego's theoretical framework.
*   **Cluster Tags:** Disinformation, Electoral Integrity, Fact-Checking, Brazil, Superior Electoral Court (TSE), Judicial Response, Populism, WhatsApp, LatAm.
*   **Possible Citation Uses:** Comparing judicial vs. legislative responses to misinformation; analyzing the role of fact-checkers in non-Western democracies; discussing the institutionalization of truth verification.
*   **Limits:** Single-country focus (Brazil); data relies on TSE's own verification platform (selection bias); qualitative sample size is moderate (6 interviews).
*   **Open Questions:** Long-term impact of TSE-public collaboration on voter trust; comparative effectiveness of judicial vs. state-led fact-checking models.

### Risks, Ethics, and Evidence
*   **Risks Named:** Manipulation (deliberate deception), polarization (erosion of public debate), opacity (undermining electoral institution legitimacy), and privacy (illicit phone number lists used for WhatsApp groups).
*   **Empirical Evidence vs. Speculation:** The source provides empirical evidence on the *targets* of disinformation (TSE vs. Candidates) and the *volume* of attacks. It cites external researchers (Bastos & Recuero, 2023) regarding "coup implications" as a secondary observation rather than primary claim.
*   **Inherent Harm:** The text frames disinformation as a "deliberate act" to maximize utility, but does not argue that microtargeting or social media use is *inherently* harmful; rather, it highlights the *exploitation* of these tools by right-wing populism.
*   **Roles:** Platforms (partners/collaborators like Google/WhatsApp), Campaign (Bolsonaro's team), Regulator (TSE acting as a non-regulatory authority).
*   **Counterargument:** The text acknowledges that legislative regulation to counter misinformation carries risks regarding the "determination of 'truth'" and press freedom, which is why the TSE chose a non-legislative path.
*   **Framing Sentence:** "The study distinguishes fear from evidence by employing longitudinal quantitative data to identify specific vulnerabilities (voting system vs. court integrity) while acknowledging the lack of robust regulatory frameworks that might otherwise be abused."
