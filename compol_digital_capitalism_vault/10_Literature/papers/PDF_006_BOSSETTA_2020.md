### **1. Bibliographic Identity & Source Analysis**

*   **Bibliographic Identity:**
    *   **Author:** Michael Bossetta.
    *   **Title:** Scandalous Design: How Social Media Platforms’ Responses to Scandal Impacts Campaigns and Elections.
    *   **Journal:** *Social Media + Society*.
    *   **Publication Date:** 2020 (Issue 1, Pages 1–4).
    *   **Article Type:** Conceptual/Theoretical Essay (Research Article).
    *   **DOI:** 10.1177/2056305120924777.
*   **What the Source Studies:**
    *   The programmatic design changes social media platforms make in response to public scandals (e.g., Cambridge Analytica, state-sponsored disinformation).
    *   The concept of "Scandalous Design" as a mechanism where platforms position themselves as political actors rather than neutral channels.
*   **Method:**
    *   **Theoretical/Conceptual Analysis:** Introduces a new typology ("Scandalous Design").
    *   **Illustrative Empirical Examples:** Uses specific case studies (Ad Archives, WhatsApp forwarding limits, Facebook hiring, Twitter bot removal) to buttress the theoretical framework.

    *   Recent empirical examples from elections in the **United States, European Union, India, Brazil, and China.**
    *   Industry data (Facebook Ad Library, Google Transparency Report).

    *   Social media companies should be studied not just as tools for political actors, but as **political actors in their own right.**
    *   **Scandalous Design:** Platforms introduce or manipulate features (architectural), and alter analog (organizational) and digital (policy) protocols in response to scandal to mitigate consequences and generate goodwill with governments.
    *   Design choices (e.g., keyword search requirements in Ad Archives) represent strategic positioning and control over data transparency.

### **2. Paid Ads vs. Organic Posts & Platform Mechanics**

*   **Paid vs. Organic:**
    *   **Primary Focus:** **Paid Ads** (Political Ad Archives, Ad Library, Ad targeting strategies).
    *   **Organic:** Mentioned only as a benchmark for comparison. The text states studies of political ads "need to be benchmarked to other forms of digital campaigning, either through rigorous cross-platform research or comparisons between paid and organic social media content."
*   **Platform:**
    *   Facebook, Google, WhatsApp, Twitter, YouTube, Pinterest.
*   **Targeting Mechanism:**
    *   **Microtargeting:** Cited as a concern driving the need for transparency.
    *   **Ad Archives:** Allow reverse-engineering of targeting strategies (e.g., looking at usage spikes to infer "testing periods").
*   **Ad Visibility:**
    *   **Ad Archives:** Create visibility but are limited by "keyword search requirement" (users/researchers must search specific terms to see data).
*   **Disclosure:**
    *   Platforms disclose ad data through "Ad Library" but strategically exclude aggregate-level data to maintain business interests.
*   **Persuasion:**
    *   Platforms manipulate features to influence information environments (e.g., WhatsApp forwarding limits to stop disinformation; Google autocomplete changes).
*   **Segmentation:**
    *   Implied through "microtargeting" strategies campaigns use, which can be inferred via ad archives.
*   **Audience Control:**
    *   Platforms control access to data via API accessibility and search filters (e.g., preventing access to aggregate-level data in ad archives).

### **4. Risks, Evidence, & Ethics**

*   **Risks Named:**
    *   Manipulation, Disinformation, Polarization, Privacy, Violence (lynch mobs in India), Unequal Exposure, Opacity (in ad archives).
*   **Empirical Evidence vs. Speculation:**
    *   **Design Changes:** Treated as empirical evidence of political positioning (e.g., Facebook hiring moderators, WhatsApp limiting forwards).
    *   **Electoral Impact:** Some claims are speculative regarding the *full* impact of design changes on election outcomes, relying on the argument that "scholars risk overlooking a key mechanism."
*   **Microtargeting Harm:**
    *   **Inherently Harmful?** No, not inherently. The concern is the **lack of transparency** surrounding it. The text suggests ad archives (transparency) mitigate the opacity risk.
*   **Platform/Campaign/Regulator Roles:**
    *   **Platforms:** Lobbying, design changes, self-governance protocols.
    *   **Campaigns:** Use ad archives for opposition research; adjust spending based on competitor data.
    *   **Regulators:** Pressure platforms to change design (e.g., government ire leading to Ad Libraries).
*   **Counterargument:**
    *   The text acknowledges that "deficiencies" exist in ad archives (e.g., keyword limits) and that platforms may use them strategically to control what is seen ("strategic positioning through design").
*   **Ethics/Evidence Debate Framing Sentence:**
    *   "Without cognizance of how platforms’ operation—both digitally as products and politically as actors—evolves in response to scandal, scholars risk overlooking a key mechanism that contextualizes social media’s role in contemporary elections."

### **5. Paper-Specific Questions (G1, G3, G5)**

**[G1_PAID_ADS_OBJECT]**
*   **Paid vs. Organic:** The source primarily studies **Paid Ads** (specifically political ad archives and transparency reports). It mentions organic content only as a necessary benchmark for comparison ("comparisons between paid and organic social media content").
*   **Platform:** Facebook, Google, WhatsApp, Twitter.
*   **Targeting Mechanism:** **Microtargeting** is the primary concern driving the creation of ad archives. Platforms use targeting to deliver ads; researchers use archives to reverse-engineer this targeting.
*   **Visibility/Disclosure:** Visibility is partial. Ad Archives provide a "glimpse" but require specific **keyword searches**, preventing access to aggregate-level data.
*   **Persuasion/Segmentation:** The source notes that design changes (like WhatsApp forwards) limit the spread of disinformation (persuasion control). Ad archives allow campaigns to segment and target based on opponent data.
*   **Audience Control:** Platforms control audience access to data via API restrictions and search filters (e.g., keyword requirements in Ad Library).

**[G3_PLATFORMIZATION_DIGITAL_POLITICS]**
*   **Definition:** **Platformization** is framed as the recognition of platforms as **political actors** rather than just digital channels. "Scandalous Design" is the mechanism where platforms alter architecture/protocols to respond to political scandals.
*   **Mechanisms:** Introduction of new features (Ad Libraries), manipulation of existing features (WhatsApp forwards), analog protocols (hiring policy staff), and digital protocols (bot removal).
*   **Framing:** Platforms are treated as **Active Infrastructures/Political Actors**, not passive channels. They encode strategy into products.
*   **Geographic Scope:** Comparative/Global (US, EU, India, Brazil, China).
*   **Mechanisms Linking Platforms to Strategy:** Design choices (e.g., allowing altered videos to remain, removing coordinated campaigns) signal political stances and influence campaigning environments.
*   **Chile 2021 Bridge Sentence:** *Not available in text.*

**[G5_ETHICS_MANIPULATION_RISK]**
*   **Democratic Risk Identified:** **Disinformation**, **Polarization**, **Privacy** (via data control), **Manipulation** (via ad targeting opacity), and **Violence** (via WhatsApp forwarding).
*   **Concern Type:** Focuses heavily on **Opacity** (in ad archives) and **Manipulation** (via design tweaks to stop disinformation or spread it).
*   **Fear vs. Evidence:** The source treats the *existence* of design changes as evidence of political strategy, but warns that without understanding these changes, scholars may miss the *mechanism* of influence. It distinguishes between the fear of "microtargeting" (which drives transparency tools) and the reality of "strategic positioning" (which drives design).
*   **Role of Ethics:** Ethics is framed through the "trust" relationship between platforms and regulators/citizens. Platforms generate goodwill via design changes (e.g., removing bots) but maintain control via data access restrictions.
