### Bibliographic Identity & Core Extraction

**Bibliographic Identity**
*   **Authors:** Ernesto Calvo & Tiago Ventura
*   **Title:** Will I Get COVID-19? Partisanship, Social Media Frames, and Perceptions of Health Risk in Brazil
*   **Journal:** *Latin American Politics and Society* (Volume 63, Issue 1, 2021)
*   **DOI:** 10.1017/lap.2020.30

**What the Source Studies**
*   **Subject:** Partisan differences in health and job risk perceptions during the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in Brazil.
*   **Focus:** How elite political frames (specifically President Bolsonaro's rhetoric and social media messages) influence subjective risk assessment among supporters and opposition voters.

**Method and Corpus**
*   **Methodology:** Mixed methods.
    1.  **Survey:** Fielded March 23 – May 8, 2020 (embedded within a larger project).
    2.  **Difference-in-Difference Design:** Analyzed variance in perception before vs. after President Bolsonaro's first national address on TV.
    3.  **Social Media Framing Experiment:** IRB-approved experiment exposing respondents to positive/negative social media messages about COVID-19 to measure sharing, emotion, and risk perception.
*   **Corpus:** Survey respondents from Brazil; experimental stimuli consisting of high-level politician social media messages.

**Core Claims**
*   **Partisanship as a Wedge:** Partisanship drives distinct expectations of health and job risks; progovernment (Bolsonaro) partisans report significantly lower risks than opposition supporters.
*   **Elite Influence:** Bolsonaro's speech increased risk perception among opposition voters but not government partisans (updating partisan alignment).
*   **Framing Effects:** Experimental social media frames had modest effects on risk perception.
*   **Mediation Mechanism:** "Anger" mediates the link between frames and risk (frames → anger → risk), though frames did not consistently elicit anger across all groups.

**Relevance to Diego's Paper (Chile Context)**
*   **Comparative LatAm Case:** Provides empirical evidence from Brazil (another LatAm nation) regarding polarization during a health crisis, offering a contrast to Diego's likely focus on Chile 2021.
*   **Methodological Contrast:** Highlights the use of survey/experiment methods to measure political perception, which differs from Diego's likely focus on *platformization* and campaign data.
*   **Theoretical Anchor:** Validates the claim that partisan identity can override objective health facts, supporting the argument that digital/political environments in LatAm are deeply polarized.

**Possible Citation Uses**
*   **Evidence of Polarization:** Citing the divergence in risk perception between partisan groups in LatAm.
*   **Limitations of Framing:** Using the "modest framing effects" finding to argue against the assumption that social media frames alone determine outcomes (compared to structural polarization).
*   **Health Policy & Politics:** Linking political messaging to epidemiological outcomes (e.g., "districts with high support for Trump/Bolsonaro have steeper curves").

**Limits**
*   **Scope:** Limited to Brazil and the specific 2020 pandemic onset period.
*   **Mechanism:** Framing effects were found to be modest compared to the robust effect of partisan alignment.
*   **Generalizability:** Brazil's "weakly institutionalized" party system differs from Chile's distinct party fragmentation dynamics.

**Open Questions**
*   **Long-term Effects:** How do these risk perceptions evolve beyond the immediate crisis response?
*   **Platform Sensitivity:** Why were experimental social media frames less effective than elite speeches (Bolsonaro's address)?

#### [G3_PLATFORMIZATION_DIGITAL_POLITICS]
**Concept of Digitalization/Platformization:**
*   **Definition:** The source does **not** use the term "platformization" or "datafication." It focuses on **"social media frames"** and **"channels"** for elite messaging.
*   **Active vs. Passive:** Platforms are treated primarily as **channels** for disseminating political frames (passive infrastructure) rather than active infrastructures shaping behavior through algorithms (though the experiment tests frame exposure). Bolsonaro utilizes the "bully pulpit" and social media to actively challenge scientific recommendations.

**Mechanisms Linking Platforms to Strategy:**
*   **Mechanism:** Elite cues (President's speech) and social media messages alter voter perceptions of risk.
*   **Scope:** **Local/Specific.** The analysis is specific to the Brazilian political context and the specific experiment conducted, not a global or comparative study of platformization.

**Chile 2021 Bridge Sentence:**
*   *This study demonstrates how political messaging shapes risk perception in Brazil; similarly, in Chile 2021, the convergence of platformization and constitutional debate suggests that digital channels function not merely as information providers but as active arenas for partisan identity reinforcement.*

#### [G5_ETHICS_MANIPULATION_RISK]
**Democratic Risk Identified:**
*   **Primary Risk:** **Polarization** and the manipulation of public health risk perception (misinformation/opacity).
*   **Specifics:** Partisan alignment leads to rejecting scientific recommendations (masks, distancing) based on elite cues.

**Empirical Evidence vs. Speculation:**
*   **Evidence:** The study provides empirical evidence via survey and experiment showing that opposition voters increased their perceived risk after Bolsonaro's speech.
*   **Framing Effects:** Evidence for social media framing effects was found to be **modest**, suggesting that the fear of manipulation via micro-targeting frames may be over-emphasized compared to the power of partisan identity itself.

**Platform/Campaign/Regulator Roles:**
*   **Campaign/Leader Role:** Bolsonaro and the government actively impaired agencies and used the "bully pulpit" to minimize risk.
*   **Platform Role:** Treated as a channel for message dissemination; the study does not analyze platform algorithms or content moderation policies (regulator role).

**Counterargument/Ethics Debate Framing Sentence:**
*   *While the source confirms that partisan cues override objective health data, the modest effect of social media frames suggests that regulatory efforts must focus less on platform content and more on mitigating the broader effects of political polarization.*
