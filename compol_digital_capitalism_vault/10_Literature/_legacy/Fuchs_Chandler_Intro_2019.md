#### 1. Mechanisms of "Big Data Capitalism" in Facebook Advertising

Fuchs explicitly uses Facebook as a prime example of Big Data capitalism. He notes that Facebook’s massive advertising profits (e.g., $10.2 billion in 2016) are "generated by targeting users based on the analysis of Big Data generated through users’ activities and content" **(p. 5)**. The key mechanism is **algorithmic knowledge**: the ability to process massive volume, variety, and velocity of data streams in "real-time" to identify correlations and patterns **(p. 5-6)**. Facebook doesn't just host content; it actively datafies human interaction, turning behavioral patterns into predictive commodities that can be sold to political actors for micro-targeting.

#### 2. "Digital Labour" and Political Advertising Users

Fuchs draws on Harvey to emphasize that sectors like Facebook "have grown very fast on the back of free labour" **(p. 14)**. In the context of political advertising, every like, share, comment, and click a user makes is an act of **digital labour**. Users are not just the audience; they are the unpaid workers who produce the raw material (data) that Facebook refines and sells. When a political campaign uses Facebook Ads, they are purchasing the aggregated, exploited digital labour of the electorate. The users' political expressions and social behaviors are alienated from them and transformed into a commodity for capital accumulation.

#### 3. Tensions: Digital Optimism vs. Digital Pessimism in Electoral Politics

Fuchs outlines this core contradiction on **page 2**:

- **Digital Optimism** claims technologies enable "participatory culture, networked activism and distributed democracy." In elections, this looks like the promise of greater voter mobilization, grassroots fundraising, and direct candidate-to-voter communication.
- **Digital Pessimism** argues these technologies deepen domination through "networked authoritarianism, digital dehumanisation, alienation 2.0, networked exploitation and the rise of the surveillance society" **(p. 2)**. In elections, this manifests as manipulative micro-targeting, echo chambers, and the commodification of democratic participation. The tension is that the _same_ infrastructure that promises democratic participation is structurally designed for surveillance and capital extraction.

#### 🔍 Concrete Empirical Questions for Your 2021 Election Data

Based on this theoretical framing, here are specific questions to guide your empirical analysis of the Facebook Ads data:

1. **The Correlational Machine**: Do the ad targeting parameters rely on deep causal political issues, or do they exploit superficial, correlational behavioral data (e.g., targeting based on unrelated consumer habits or demographic proxies)? _(Connects to Chandler’s "correlational machine", p. 3)_.
2. **Extraction of Digital Labour**: Can we quantify the "free labour" harvested? For example, which ads generated the highest organic engagement (shares/comments), and how was that engagement subsequently leveraged or monetized by the campaign?
3. **Surveillance vs. Participation**: Does the data show a pattern of "hyper-exploitation" (Harvey, p. 13), where campaigns are extracting value from marginalized or highly specific demographic groups using invasive data points, rather than fostering broad, transparent democratic discourse?
