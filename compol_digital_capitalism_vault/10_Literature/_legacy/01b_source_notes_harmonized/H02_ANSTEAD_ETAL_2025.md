## What this source is about

This study compares paid Facebook ads to traditional TV ads during the 2017 UK General Election. It explores how digital ads are "private" and less transparent than TV, and how platform rules (like pricing and algorithms) push campaigns to focus on mobilizing their existing supporters rather than trying to persuade new voters.

## Method or approach

The researchers compared a dataset of Facebook ads (gathered through the "Who Targets Me" browser plugin) against traditional television Party Election Broadcasts (PEBs).

## Case, country, platform, and period

Country / region: United Kingdom
Election or political context: 2017 General Election
Platform: Facebook
Time period: 2017
Type of material studied: Paid digital political advertisements and traditional broadcast ads.

## Main relevance for Diego’s paper

This is a key source for the **Paid Ads (G1)** and **Democratic Risk (G5)** sections. It provides clear evidence of how platforms act as "active infrastructures" that shape campaign strategy and highlights the risk of "political redlining"—where certain groups of voters are ignored by the democratic process.

## Useful claims

- Facebook ads are "nontransparent" because they appear in private individual feeds rather than public spaces.
- The platform's pricing structure makes it cheaper for campaigns to polarize and mobilize their fans than to try to persuade undecided voters.
- "Political redlining" occurs when campaigns use data to ignore citizens they think have "little electoral value."
- Algorithms act as "hidden arbiters" that can misdirect ads or exclude certain groups regardless of what the campaign intends.
- While there is a "moral panic" about microtargeting, the study found that the actual topics discussed were mostly "politics as usual."
