# CF01_Microtargeting_As_Digital_Capitalism

## Theoretical Position (Fuchs/Digital Capitalism Lens)
From a critical theoretical perspective, grounded in Christian Fuchs' conception of digital capitalism, microtargeting practices in political campaigns instantiate the logic of platform capitalism. This perspective posits that digital capitalism is characterized by the exploitation of users' labor and data, with platforms acting as instruments of capitalist accumulation. Microtargeting, as a key component of digital political advertising, embodies this logic by leveraging user data to create highly targeted and personalized advertisements. This not only reflects the commodification of attention and the exploitation of user labor but also underscores the power asymmetries inherent in digital political infrastructure, where platforms wield significant control over the dissemination of information and the shaping of political discourse. By adopting Fuchs' digital capitalism lens, we critically examine how microtargeting practices reinforce the dominance of platform capitalism, perpetuate the exploitation of users, and influence the political economy of digital campaigns.

## Literature Integration
The integration of literature from paid digital ads (G1) and digital capitalism/platform power (G6) reveals a complex interplay between microtargeting practices, platform capitalism, and the political economy of digital campaigns. Studies such as Anstead et al. (2025) and Bossetta (2020) highlight the role of microtargeting in shaping political communication, with platforms like Facebook altering their architecture and protocols in response to public scandals, thereby exerting control over data transparency and political discourse. The work of Dobber et al. (2020) and Hameleers et al. (2020) further underscores the significance of microtargeting in amplifying the effects of political messaging, including the spread of misinformation and the manipulation of public opinion. From a digital capitalism perspective, Fuchs (2019) argues that such practices are emblematic of the exploitation of users' labor and data, with platforms commodifying attention and shaping political subjectivity. The concept of "Communicative Capitalism" introduced by Dean (2019) also resonates with the logic of microtargeting, where communication and attention are commodified, reinforcing the power dynamics of platform capitalism. Therefore, the synthesis of these literature clusters underscores the critical need to examine microtargeting practices through the lens of digital capitalism, highlighting the exploitative nature of platform capitalism and its profound implications for political communication and democracy.

## Empirical Implications for Chile 2021
The framework of microtargeting as digital capitalism offers significant empirical implications for the analysis of Gabriel Boric's and other candidates' Facebook ads in Chile 2021. By applying Fuchs' digital capitalism lens, researchers can critically examine how microtargeting practices were used to exploit user data, commodify attention, and shape political discourse during the campaign. This involves analyzing the targeting mechanisms, ad visibility, and audience control strategies employed by candidates, as well as the role of Facebook's algorithmic inference systems in categorizing users and influencing the visibility of political content. Furthermore, the study can investigate how microtargeting practices reinforced or challenged the power asymmetries inherent in digital political infrastructure, including the dominance of platform capitalism and the exploitation of user labor. By doing so, the empirical analysis can provide insights into the political economy of digital campaigns in Chile 2021, highlighting the ways in which microtargeting practices instantiate the logic of platform capitalism and shape the contours of political communication in the digital age.

## Unresolved Questions
Despite the integration of literature and the application of Fuchs' digital capitalism lens, several unresolved questions remain. These include: How do microtargeting practices intersect with other forms of digital political communication, such as organic posts and influencer marketing, to shape political discourse and influence public opinion? In what ways do platform capitalism and the exploitation of user labor reinforce or challenge existing power dynamics in political communication, and what are the implications for democracy? How can critical theories of digital capitalism inform the development of alternative, more democratic forms of digital political communication, and what role can regulatory mechanisms play in mitigating the exploitative nature of microtargeting practices? Addressing these questions requires further research and critical analysis, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of the complex interplay between microtargeting practices, platform capitalism, and the political economy of digital campaigns.
