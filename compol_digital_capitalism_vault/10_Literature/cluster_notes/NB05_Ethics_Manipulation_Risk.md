# Ethics and Manipulation Risk

## Cluster Definition
Analyzes democratic risk factors such as manipulation, polarization, privacy issues, misinformation, and unequal exposure, distinguishing empirical evidence from theoretical fear.

## Synthesis of Sources
The Ethics and Manipulation Risk cluster encompasses a broad range of democratic risk factors associated with digital politics, including manipulation, polarization, privacy issues, misinformation, and unequal exposure. A synthesis of the sources reveals a complex landscape where various actors, including politicians, campaigns, and platforms, interact to influence public opinion and democratic processes.

Anstead et al. (2025) investigated the role of paid Facebook election advertising during the 2017 UK General Election, finding that while broad demographic targeting occurs, the timing of the election campaign is the most important factor in what appears in a user's feed. This study suggests that manipulation through microtargeting may not be as effective as feared, as campaign timing outweighs specific targeting variables.

In contrast, Boulianne et al. (2024) conducted a cross-national quantitative survey analysis, demonstrating that ideology drives the adoption and political uses of social media platforms. They found that right-wing users are more likely to expose and post political content on certain platforms, highlighting the risk of polarization and echo chambers.

Cazzamatta et al. (2024) analyzed disinformation attacks against Brazil's electoral system, revealing a surge in such attacks and highlighting the role of judicial bodies in responding to misinformation. This study emphasizes the importance of institutional responses to disinformation and the need for fact-checking mechanisms.

Dobber et al. (2020) empirically demonstrated the effects of deepfakes on political attitudes, showing that self-produced political deepfakes can lower attitudes toward a depicted politician. This study highlights the risk of manipulation through microtargeting and the potential for deepfakes to undermine democratic legitimacy.

Dourado et al. (2024) investigated the typology of discursive actions of harmful political content on WhatsApp and Facebook during the 2020 Brazilian municipal elections. They identified four types of harmful speech (uncivil, conspiratorial, hateful, and dangerous) and found that these types of speech were more prevalent on Facebook than on WhatsApp.

Gil-de-Zúñiga et al. (2020) discussed the rise of populism in the era of social media, highlighting the role of social media in facilitating populist communication. They argued that social media acts as a "pivotal playing role" in populist campaigns, allowing for low-cost, viral, and emotional communication.

Hameleers et al. (2026) found that populist messages and styles communicated by politicians trigger user behavior on social media, with stylistic elements such as emotionality and first-person references being more effective than ideological content. This study highlights the risk of manipulation through emotional appeals and the potential for polarization.

Heseltine et al. (2023) analyzed the evolution of media engagement strategies by US Members of Congress on Twitter and Facebook, revealing an asymmetric polarization trend. They found that Republicans increasingly engage with right-wing and alternative media, while Democrats remain more focused on mainstream or left-leaning outlets.

Hoffmann et al. (2025) investigated the role of personality in online political engagement, finding that extraversion positively relates to political information exposure and posting on Twitch. This study highlights the importance of understanding user personality in the context of online political engagement.

Jamil-Marques et al. (2024) analyzed the impact of Bolsonaro's presidency on the Brazilian media landscape, highlighting the ways in which populists can disrupt traditional media norms. This study emphasizes the importance of understanding the complex relationships between politicians, media, and public opinion.

Klinger et al. (2023) examined the relationship between negative emotional content, dramatization, and populist messaging in political party Facebook posts across the European Parliament elections. They found that negativity, dramatization, and populist content have increased over time, and that these types of posts yield higher user engagement.

Leerssen et al. (2023) developed a tool for collecting and analyzing visual content in Facebook political ads, highlighting the importance of understanding the role of images in political communication. This study emphasizes the need for more research on the impact of visual misinformation on democratic processes.

Lukito et al. (2019) analyzed the temporal coordination of disinformation campaigns across social media platforms, revealing a pattern of coordination between Reddit and Twitter. This study highlights the risk of manipulation through coordinated disinformation campaigns.

Maurer et al. (2023) investigated the instrumentalization of news media by right-wing populist parties, finding that populists succeed by intentionally violating social norms to provoke negative media coverage. This study emphasizes the importance of understanding the complex relationships between politicians, media, and public opinion.

Schmokel et al. (2022) developed a method for analyzing visual content in Facebook political ads, highlighting the importance of understanding the role of images in political communication. This study emphasizes the need for more research on the impact of visual misinformation on democratic processes.

Thorson et al. (2019) examined the intersection of user behavior and platform algorithms, finding that Facebook's algorithmic inference systems categorize users based on political interest and influence the visibility of political and news content. This study highlights the risk of manipulation through algorithmic inference and the potential for unequal exposure to information.

Vrielink et al. (2025) investigated the influence of targeted online political advertising on voters' perceived issue importance, finding that exposure to online ads increases the salience of issues among voters. This study highlights the risk of manipulation through targeted advertising and the potential for agenda-setting effects.

Yang et al. (2023) analyzed the prevalence and types of visual misinformation on Facebook during the 2020 US general election, finding that visual misinformation is highly prevalent and partisan asymmetric. This study emphasizes the importance of understanding the role of visual misinformation in democratic processes and the need for more research on the impact of visual falsehoods on public opinion.

In summary, the Ethics and Manipulation Risk cluster highlights the complex and multifaceted nature of democratic risk factors in digital politics. The sources reveal a range of risks, including manipulation, polarization, privacy issues, misinformation, and unequal exposure, and emphasize the need for more research on the impact of digital politics on democratic processes.

## Gaps and Tensions
The literature in the Ethics and Manipulation Risk cluster reveals several gaps and tensions. One key tension is the debate over the effectiveness of microtargeting in manipulating public opinion. While some studies suggest that microtargeting can be effective in influencing voter behavior, others argue that its impact is limited.

Another gap is the lack of research on the impact of visual misinformation on democratic processes. While there is a growing body of research on the prevalence and types of visual misinformation, more research is needed on its effects on public opinion and democratic outcomes.

Furthermore, there is a need for more research on the role of personality in online political engagement. While some studies have investigated the relationship between personality traits and online political behavior, more research is needed to fully understand this complex relationship.

Finally, there is a tension between the need for more research on the impact of digital politics on democratic processes and the need for immediate action to address the risks associated with digital politics. While more research is needed to fully understand the complex relationships between digital politics, public opinion, and democratic outcomes, there is also a need for policymakers and regulators to take action to address the risks associated with digital politics, such as manipulation, polarization, and misinformation.
